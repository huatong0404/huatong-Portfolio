An experiment in making room to grow

On campus, we set an unplanted plywood structure beside the lawn. From a distance, its triangular faces turned as one shape. Up close, the slots and connectors made its construction plain.

Pollen Farm began with a question: how could flat-cut pieces become a garden that grows in more than one direction? We built one full-scale configuration to test the geometry and assembly. The garden in the drawings remains a proposal.

01 / Paper studies

What if the gap mattered as much as the solid?

We began with paper because a small form could be folded, held, and rearranged. The empty spaces between its faces were easier to notice in a hand than in a flat drawing.

What we noticed

A repeatable unit would need to keep making room, rather than closing into one fixed mass.

From flat

The net showed the faces as cuttable parts, before they occupied space.

To cluster

Moving units together changed the outline and the gaps at the same time.

02 / The growth rule

The form could change because the rule stayed clear.

Paper gave us a question about space. The drawings helped us choose a family of modules and describe the move that lets one become many.

02 / 03 — the move

Mirror, then fold.

The diagram begins with one module. We mirror it along an edge and fold it over that edge, making a connection that can be repeated.

Why it mattered

A simple local move can produce a changing overall silhouette.

03 / Connections

On paper, two edges simply touch. Plywood asks how.

If a unit is assembled from triangles, the seams must remain repeatable too.

04 / Assembly

The drawing became a set of decisions about build order.

When the joints worked at small scale, we needed to understand how separate panels would become modules, then how those modules would meet.

From drawing to site

The diagram organized the parts. The full-size build would show whether those relationships still made sense at the scale of a person.

05 / Built and imagined

We built the structure. The garden still needs a living test.

The finished plywood form let us check the geometry, the joints, and its presence on campus. Planting remained an invitation in the drawings and composite scenes.

Built record / imagined use

Switch views to see what we made and what we proposed.

01 / 02 — what we tested

The rule stood at full scale.

The plywood parts were assembled into one configuration and placed beside the lawn. Here we could inspect its turning profile and visible joints at the scale of a person.

Look closer: the slots and small connectors remain visible.

What the next version must answer

Where would soil sit? How would water drain? Which plants belong here, and who would care for them through changing weather? Those questions need plants and time, not another rendering.

06 / Reflection

From a rule to a responsibility

I began by treating computational design as a way to generate form. By the end, I cared more about whether a rule could survive translation: from paper to drawing, from drawing to joint, from joint to plywood, and from plywood into a place people would pass.

We assembled one configuration, not the ecology promised by the name Pollen Farm. To become a garden, the project would need another kind of collaboration—with plants, water, weather, pollinators, and whoever keeps it alive.
