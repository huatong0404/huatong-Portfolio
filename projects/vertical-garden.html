Pollen Farm

CategoryProduct Design · Digital Fabrication
RoleDesigner & Fabricator
ContextCMU Fundamentals of Computational Design, Spring 2024
TeamLisa, Catalina, Kai, Hua, Bleona

How can a flat sheet become a living, spatial system? Pollen Farm turns one repeatable polyhedral module into a human-scale vertical garden. A rule-based geometry, custom joints, and digital fabrication allow the structure to grow without hiding how it is made.

Design questionMake a modular garden that can aggregate in multiple directions.
SystemTriangular plywood panels, five connector types, and mirrored modules.
OutcomeA 3′9″-tall prototype with planting pockets and room to expand.

From Form to Rule

We began with paper polyhedra rather than a fixed silhouette. By comparing triangular, quadrilateral, pentagonal, and hexagonal bases, we could test how each family balanced enclosure, stability, and the surface area available for planting.

The selected module could be mirrored along an edge and folded over another face. That simple operation produced a repeatable grammar: the garden could become taller, wider, or denser while preserving the same construction logic.

Unit

A faceted module built from flat triangular panels.

Operation

Mirror, fold, connect, and repeat along shared faces.

Geometry Studies

Physical studies made the aggregation rules tangible before we committed to full-scale material.

Designing the Joint

The form only works if the connection system can absorb several meeting angles. Instead of relying on glue or a hidden frame, we designed four legible tab connectors. Each one corresponds to a specific edge condition, turning geometric relationships into parts that can be cut, sorted, and assembled.

The joint system keeps the panels removable and lets the construction remain visible as part of the object’s character.

Prototype, Test, Refine

Laser-cut models moved the project from diagram to assembly. We tested connector tolerances at individual seams, then increased the number of panels until the modules could support one another as a larger structure.

Assembly as a Sequence

The exploded axonometric clarified the build order. Individual panels first close into a module; finished modules then meet at shared edges. Separating these two scales of assembly reduced ambiguity and gave the team a common guide during fabrication.

Color-coded joints in the assembled axonometric make each connection type easy to locate and audit.

The Full-Scale Proposal

The final form stacks three clusters into a porous vertical landscape. Openings hold flowering plants while the changing orientation of each face creates pockets of shade, exposure, and visual depth.

In Context

Placed on campus, the object reads differently from every approach: as a marker at a distance, a layered geometric body up close, and a planted micro-landscape when viewed through its openings.

Reflection

This project changed my understanding of computational design: the strongest outcome was not one complicated form, but a clear system capable of producing many forms. The matrix, mirroring rule, and joint families made every decision traceable while leaving room for variation.

Building at full scale also exposed what drawings could not—plywood flex, tolerance at repeated joints, assembly order, and the way gaps between panels naturally become planting pockets. Those discoveries turned geometric constraints into the most useful features of the garden.
