JamSesh

Category Physical Computing · Industrial Design
Role Industrial Design & Sound Engineering
Context CMU, Spring 2025
Team Jacqueline, Aimee, Hua

JamSesh is a collaborative sound-making device designed for teenagers. Inspired by the theremin, it lets players create music through body gestures — tilting to change pitch, pressing to trigger rhythm, and moving closer to friends to adjust volume. No musical training required.

Team: Jacqueline · Aimee · Hua

Problem Space

Teenagers face a rising sense of loneliness, amplified by the pandemic and social media. Traditional in-person interactions — hanging out at friends' houses, forming high-school bands — have declined. Our research identified a need for off-screen, explorative play that lowers the embarrassment barrier and encourages face-to-face connection in third spaces.

Why Music?

Face-to-face verbal communication can feel too serious for teens. Inspired by high-school bands, we designed an instrument-speaker system that values fun over skill. Unlike traditional instruments, JamSesh offers a relaxed, explorative way of making sound — where instruments react to each other, encouraging collaboration without requiring everyone to be at the same skill level.

Form Exploration

The form needed to suggest tilt — a horizontal reference to the ground. We explored symmetrical shapes with a large, prominent button. Early iterations were too small or suggested incorrect handholds. Through CAD modeling and physical prototyping, we converged on an organic, pebble-like form that fits naturally in the hand.

Final Design

The final instrument features a silicone play button, a scroll wheel for instrument selection, and a gyroscope for tilt-based pitch control. The companion speaker doubles as a charging dock, with an on/off switch, microphone, and record button. Instruments charge and store by magnetically attaching to the speaker.

Technical Prototype

Each instrument uses an ESP32-C6 with a gyroscope for pitch, BLE proximity sensing for volume, a button for rhythm, and a rotary potentiometer for instrument selection. Data streams via BLE to a multithreaded Python script that filters inputs and processes output through pydub for real-time pitch remapping.

Reflection

JamSesh taught me that designing for play means designing for vulnerability — teenagers won't engage with something that makes them feel awkward. The key insight was that music, unlike conversation, gives people permission to be imperfect together.

From a technical standpoint, transitioning from Arduino to ESP32-C6 mid-project was a major pivot. The multithreading challenges forced us to rethink our entire data pipeline, but the result was a more responsive, scalable system that could handle multiple simultaneous inputs without latency.
