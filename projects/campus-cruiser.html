Campus Cruiser

Category UI/UX · Interaction Design
Role Fabrication Director · UX Researcher · Interface Designer
Context CMU HCI Department, Fall 2024
Team John, Xinfei, Daisy, Hua
[Hero image — high-fidelity e-bike UI mockup on device, or product render of the Campus Cruiser e-bike with screen interface visible]

A semi-autonomous e-bike designed for college students, featuring a student ID-based rental system, intuitive physical controls, and a screen interface for mode switching, turn signals, and speed adjustment. The design prioritizes clarity, ease of use, and safety for efficient campus mobility.

Team: John · Xinfei · Daisy · Hua

Design Task

Our team aimed to create an intuitive and user-friendly interface for an e-bike with semi-autonomous functionality, allowing riders to partially disengage from active control during the ride. The design prioritizes clarity, ease of use, and safety, ensuring that users can seamlessly interact with the system while maintaining confidence in its operation.

[Image — e-bike concept sketch or overview diagram showing the semi-autonomous vehicle design]

User Research

Our research combined primary and secondary methods. We conducted 3 on-campus interviews with e-bike riders near CMU to understand their experiences and pain points. Key findings:

  • ~50% of rental bikes were broken or low on battery
  • Poor seat-handlebar alignment caused discomfort
  • Riders wanted clear issue indicators and lightweight, safe designs
  • E-bike adoption is growing, with bike-sharing projected to reach $12.66B by 2029
[Image — user research affinity clustering / interview notes / Bulls' Eye diagram]
Parking & Locking Issues: The locking mechanism was unclear and interfered with switching between modes.
Turn Signals & Controls: Turn signal buttons were not easily distinguishable from other controls.
SAV Mode Confusion: Users were unclear about "Auto" meaning, expecting it to pedal automatically.
Acceleration: Users struggled with the push-forward / pull-back system for speed control.
Reporting Issues: The broken bike button was rarely noticed or used by testers.

Low-Fidelity Prototype

We conducted 5 usability tests with the first iteration. The e-bike allows users to switch modes, adjust speed, and activate turn signals using physical buttons on the prototype. An electronic screen provides real-time status updates, ensuring clear communication of changes.

[Image — low-fidelity physical prototype with buttons and screen, or GIF of usability testing in action]

First Iteration

Based on usability testing, we made several improvements — especially on the physical aspect and operations. We redesigned the position of the bell and the acceleration button, and optimized the size of the interface to make it more accessible. The physical controls were rearranged for natural hand positioning during riding.

Safe Physical Control

The e-bike's autonomous system detects environmental changes and automatically slows down to prevent collisions. In extreme situations, it alerts the rider for human takeover, ensuring safety and accident prevention. This intelligent response system enhances rider confidence and reliability in semi-autonomous mode.

[Image / GIF — collision detection alert UI, showing automatic slowdown and rider notification]

Unique Locking System

The e-bike features a unique start and lock system. If the bike is taken outside the designated parking area, it alerts users with a notification. The iterated version replaced the long-press lock with a double-click operation for a more intuitive experience. Lock-screen notifications inform users when the bike is not in a ridable status.

Design System

The interface follows a cohesive design system built for clarity at speed — large touch targets, high-contrast status indicators, and a minimal color palette optimized for outdoor visibility. Components are designed for glanceability: a rider should understand the bike's state in under one second.

Reflection

This project taught me that designing for motion is fundamentally different from designing for screens at rest. Every interaction needs to be completable in seconds, with one hand, while moving. The locking system iteration — from student ID typing to long-press to double-click — is a perfect case study in reducing cognitive load through design.

As Fabrication Director, I bridged the gap between digital interface design and physical prototype construction. The biggest challenge was translating screen-based interactions into tangible button layouts that felt natural on handlebars. Usability testing with 5 participants revealed problems we never anticipated — like users confusing "Auto" mode with automatic pedaling — reinforcing that assumption-testing must happen early and often.
